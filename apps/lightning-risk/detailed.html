Detailed risk assessment — not the simplified method

Does this structure actually need lightning protection?

NFPA 780 Annex L answers that question twice. Section L.5 is a one-page screening test. Section L.6 — the one this page implements — is the real calculation: eight separate risk components, four kinds of loss, and about forty inputs. It is worth doing properly, and it is entirely doable once somebody tells you what each input means and where to find it. That is what every panel below does.

Lightning risk is not "how often will this building be struck". It is how often something bad happens because of lightning, expressed as a probability per year, and compared against a tolerable level. NFPA 780 builds that number from three ingredients, multiplied together, eight times over:

SymbolIngredientWhat it means in plain language
NThreat of occurrenceHow many lightning events per year land somewhere that matters — on the building, near it, on a cable coming in, or near that cable.
PProbability of damageGiven that event, the chance it actually causes the damage in question. This is where your protection measures earn their keep — an LPS, SPDs, bonding, shielding all push P down.
LLossGiven that damage, the fraction of the "value at stake" that is lost — lives, service continuity, heritage, or money.

Multiply them and you get a risk component: RX = NX · PX · LX (§L.6.6). There are eight of them, and they are grouped by where the lightning hit and what it broke:

Comp.Lightning hits……and causes
RAthe structureinjury or death from touch and step voltages
RBthe structurephysical damage — usually fire — to the structure
RCthe structurefailure of internal electrical / electronic systems
RMthe ground near the structurefailure of internal systems, by induction
RUan incoming service (cable, line)injury from touch voltage on that service
RVan incoming servicephysical damage where the surge enters
RWan incoming servicefailure of internal systems
RZthe ground near a servicefailure of internal systems, by induction on the line

Those eight components are then added into four risks, each with its own tolerable limit from Table L.6.2. If a risk exceeds its limit, protection is recommended — and the component breakdown tells you exactly which measure will fix it.

What you should collect before you start

A plot plan with building length, width and height · the site coordinates · a single-line diagram showing every cable and line entering the structure, with route lengths · whether an HV/LV transformer sits upstream · cable types (shielded? in conduit? buried?) · the impulse withstand rating of the most fragile equipment inside (your DCS or PLC I/O cards) · the structural column or rebar spacing · what fire detection and suppression exists · roughly how many people are inside and for how many hours a year.

01

Lightning flash density, Ng

§L.2

Every one of the eight risk components is proportional to Ng, the number of cloud-to-ground flashes per square kilometre per year. Annex L points you at a Vaisala map printed at page size (Figure L.2), which is fine for a screening test and hopeless for a defensible number. The map is built from NOAA's National Lightning Detection Network, and NOAA publishes the underlying counts for free — so this page goes to the source instead of reading a contour off a picture.

The method, in one line

NOAA's NCEI aggregates every detected flash into fixed 0.1° × 0.1° tiles — about 11 km north-south by 9 km east-west, roughly 100 km². Ask it for the daily flash count in your tile over ten years, add them up, divide by the tile's area and by ten. That is Ng, traceable to a public dataset with a URL you can paste into a report.

02

The structure

§L.4, §L.6.6.1.1–.2

A building does not only catch the flashes aimed at its footprint. It reaches outward and intercepts flashes that would otherwise have hit the ground nearby — the standard models that reach as a 1-in-3 slope from the top of the structure down to grade, so the catchment extends 3H in every direction. That is the equivalent collection area AD, and because H appears squared, height matters far more than footprint.

A second, much larger circle — what AM actually is

AD is the ground area in which a flash is captured by the structure: the footprint grown outward by 3H, because a tall building reaches sideways for a leader. AM answers a different question — where can a flash land on open ground, miss the building entirely, and still induce a damaging surge in the wiring inside it? §L.6.6.1.2 fixes that reach at 1,640 ft (500 m) beyond the perimeter, regardless of height, and counts those flashes as NM = Ng·(AM − AD)·CD·10⁻⁶ — AD is subtracted because a flash that hits the building is already counted in ND. The 500 m band alone is about 0.9 km², so AM typically runs 50 to 100 times AD and NM is usually the largest threat frequency in the assessment. It only turns into risk if your internal systems are poorly shielded, which is what Step 04 is about.

Both areas can be typed in directly in ft² instead of being derived from L, W and H — offset the real footprint by 3H for AD, or by 1,640 ft (500 m) for AM, in CAD, and enter the enclosed area. L and W stay on screen as long as either area is still being calculated, because they feed both.

And a third area — what ADJ actually is

ADJ is not another area around your building. It is the other building's own AD — its footprint grown outward by 3HJ — and it enters your assessment only because a power or signal cable runs between the two. A flash captured by that structure injects current into the shared conductor, which delivers it to your equipment: §L.6.6.1.4 counts those events as NDJ = Ng·ADJ·CDJ·CT·10⁻⁶ and adds them to the strikes on the line itself. Three areas, three questions: a flash hits you (AD), a flash lands near you (AM), a flash hits the far-end building and is conducted to you (ADJ). Like the other two, it takes a CAD-measured area in ft² if the neighbouring footprint is irregular.

03

Incoming services

§L.6.6.1.3–.5

Every conductor entering the structure is a lightning path. The standard treats each as a strip of ground: AL = 40 · LL is the strip in which a flash hits the line directly, and AI = 4000 · LL is the much wider band in which a flash to earth induces a surge onto it. Add one entry per service — typically the incoming power feeder and one signal or telecom line.

If you do not know the route length

§L.6.6.1.3 tells you to assume LL = 1000 m. That is the honest default and the one loaded here. Measuring the real route is one of the highest-value refinements in the whole assessment, because NL and NI scale directly with it.

Induced surges: the KS chain

PM — failure of internal systems from a nearby flash — is not read off a table directly. You build a shielding factor KS = KS1 · KS2 · KS3 · KS4 out of four things: how well the outer structure screens the field, whether there is an inner screened room, how the internal cabling is run, and how tough the equipment is. Table L.6.7.5 then converts KS into PM, and it is brutally steep — KS of 0.4 gives PM = 1, while 0.013 gives 0.0001. Four orders of magnitude hang on cable routing and column spacing.

05

Consequences

§L.6.6.3, Tables L.6.7.9–.13

The loss factors answer: if the damage happens, how much of what is at stake is actually lost? They are fractions, and they are small — a fire in an industrial building is assumed to cost 5 % of the total value at risk (LF = 5×10⁻²), and lightning kills someone inside a building in one case out of a hundred thousand (LT = 10⁻⁵). Then four modifiers push those numbers up or down for your specific site.
